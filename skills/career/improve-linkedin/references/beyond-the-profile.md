# Beyond the profile: network, outreach, commenting, posting, paid tiers

Read this when the person's goal needs more than a better profile, when they ask about posting or "the algorithm", or when they bring a post, a comment, a connection request or a message to improve. Sections run from cheapest and best-evidenced to most expensive and least-evidenced; recommend in that order.

Facts as of November 2026; feed behaviour changes yearly, so treat anything about reach as provisional. Untagged guidance is [practice]. Other tags: [documented] LinkedIn's own statements or help pages; [measured] independent or peer-reviewed data; [vendor] a number from LinkedIn marketing or a vendor with a stake; [contested] sources disagree; [inference] reasoning from documented facts.

## 1. Should this person be active at all?

Start here, before any tactic. No independent study ties posting to jobs landed or clients won [measured gap]. What exists is LinkedIn-commissioned survey data on buyer attitudes, which measures what buyers say, not revenue [vendor: LinkedIn B2B creator research, 2025]. So "do not post" is a legitimate recommendation, and often the right one. Say so plainly, because the person has usually been told the opposite by people who sell posting help.

Decide by goal and by where the reader decides:

| Person | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Credential-driven job seeker (law, medicine, finance, academia, regulated roles, ladders inside large employers) | Network and targeted outreach first, a few good comments second, posting optional and low priority | Hiring runs on credentials, applications, referrals and recruiters. A post adds little that the credential does not already prove. [inference] from the weak-ties and referral evidence below |
| Proof-of-work job seeker (software, design, data, writing, marketing, trades with portfolios) | Occasional post or comment that shows real work; link or describe a concrete artefact | Work samples can substitute for a missing credential. Moderate effort, not mandatory |
| Consultant or freelancer | Comments in the threads buyers read, plus a steady modest posting rhythm, if buyers are on LinkedIn | Buyers decide on trust before any call. Strongest plausible payoff, still unproven. One post a week sustained beats a burst that stops |
| Founder | Post about the problem your category cares about, narrowly and consistently | Helps hiring, fundraising and early sales when the audience is in your category. Same unproven-outcomes caveat |
| Sales or business development | Outreach and commenting first; posting only if the prospect list actually reads it | The work is already one-to-one; content is a warm-up, not a substitute |
| Someone with nothing specific to say yet | Do not post. Follow, read, comment occasionally when they have a real point | A feed that infers expertise from profile and activity gives little to generic content, and LinkedIn is reducing reach for generic posts (section 7) |

Check the situating answers first: if the reader does not use LinkedIn (many trades, many regional markets, many senior buyers), none of this section matters and the effort belongs elsewhere.

## 2. Network

Network work is the cheapest lever with the best evidence. It is also the least visible, which is why people skip it.

### Weak ties: what was tested, what it means

Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson and Aral (Science, 2022) ran randomised experiments on LinkedIn's People You May Know across about 20 million users over five years (about 2 billion new ties, about 600,000 new jobs) [measured, LinkedIn-run, peer reviewed]. Job transmission followed an inverted U: moderately weak ties (a peak around 10 mutual connections, per MIT's summary) helped most; the weakest and the strongest ties helped less. Weak ties mattered more in digital industries.

What the study tested: which suggested connections led to jobs. It did not test messages, posts or outreach. The translation is [inference]:
- Prioritise second-degree people in the target field, alumni, and people at target employers with a handful of shared contacts.
- Do not spend effort only on close friends (too overlapping with what you already hear) or on total strangers (too little shared context to be trusted).
- In non-digital industries the effect was weaker, so do not oversell it to a person in one.

### Referrals

Referred candidates are hired more, with a small initial pay premium and lower churn at the firm studied [measured: New York Fed staff report, Brown, Setren and Topa]; NBER field experiments show referrals carry real information about productivity [measured]. A recruiting vendor's claim of 4.5x hire likelihood for referred candidates (Paradigm, over 2 million candidates) is [vendor]. None of this measures LinkedIn messages specifically. [practice]: a warm referral beats a cold application, so a good outcome of networking is a named person willing to forward you.

### Network distance and being found

LinkedIn states it cannot guarantee a search result order, that results are tailored to the searcher, that closer connections (2nd versus 3rd degree) make a profile more likely to surface, and that more connections raises the odds of sharing one with a given searcher [documented]. Mutual-connection weighting beyond that is undocumented. Consequence: a connection base in the reader's own field raises the chance of being found by that reader, which matters more than the raw count.

### Follow versus connect

Following gives you someone's posts without a request and is not limited the way invitations are; connecting gives search proximity and messaging access. No dataset compares them [practice]. Follow first when the person is senior or you have no reason yet; connect when you have a specific reason and a note that states it.

### Invitation notes

Vendor data (Belkins and Expandi, 11.5 million requests, 2025) points one way but disagrees on numbers [vendor, contested]:
- One Belkins page: acceptance 25.3% with a note versus 27.6% without; reply rate after acceptance 8.2% versus 5.3%.
- Another page citing the same sources: acceptance about equal (26.42% versus 26.37%), replies 9.36% versus 5.44%.
- Waalaxy (about 2,000 sends per variant) and AISDR small tests: blank requests accepted slightly more often; notes drew more replies after acceptance [vendor].

Convergent read [inference]: a note changes acceptance little, maybe slightly downward, and raises replies after acceptance. Every sample is cold sales prospects reached by outreach tools, not job seekers, so use it as direction only. Practical rule: write a note when you have a specific reason for this person; send it blank when you do not, rather than a generic line.

### Limits that are and are not official

- 30,000 first-degree connections; followers are uncapped [documented]. Irrelevant to nearly everyone.
- Free accounts get a small monthly allowance of personalised invitation notes, Premium more [documented], and the allowance has changed before: check the person's own account.
- LinkedIn publishes no weekly invitation limit. Figures circulating (about 100 a week, higher for paid tiers, with conflicting reset rules) come from tool vendors [contested]. Never quote one as a safe number. A person sending a few considered invitations is nowhere near any limit; the practical advice is to send fewer, more specific requests.

## 3. Outreach

### What the public data covers

Almost all of it is recruiters and sellers messaging outward [vendor]. For a candidate writing to a hiring manager, or a freelancer writing to a buyer, there is no dataset. What follows is a mix of LinkedIn's own recruiter-side findings, vendor reply rates, and [practice].

Figures with provenance:
- LinkedIn publishes no current average InMail response rate. Numbers circulating are vendor claims.
- LinkedIn analysis of recruiter InMails (2019 to 2021, so old): messages under 400 characters responded about 22% above average; candidates signalling Open to Work responded about 37% more [documented, dated].
- Belkins and Expandi 2025: reply rates by recipient group about 10.9% for HR and Talent, 7.0% for C-level, 6.4% for founders; a follow-up after connecting drew about 17.6% replies from connected prospects [vendor, aggregated, inconsistent denominators].
- AI-assisted outreach versus not: 7.3% versus 7.0% reply, an uncontrolled 0.3 point gap [vendor]. Read as no meaningful difference.

### What makes a cold message get an answer

No study isolates the causes for job seekers. The pattern practitioners agree on, and LinkedIn's own "short beats long" supports [practice]:

1. A specific reason for this person: something they wrote, built, said or decided, or a shared context, in one clause. This proves the message was not sent to a list.
2. One small ask: a question answerable in a sentence, or a short call, never "pick my brain" or "any openings?".
3. An easy way out: say it is fine to ignore or redirect. It lowers the cost of replying and signals respect.

Keep it short enough to read on a phone without scrolling. Lead with the reason, not with who you are. Say who you are in one clause, because the reader will open your profile next: make sure it agrees with what you said (gate: BELIEVED).

Whether to ask for a call in message one or ask a question first has no data. Default to a question when you have no relationship, a short call when the person has invited contact.

### Worked example: job seeker to someone at a target employer

Situation: a logistics analyst wants a planning role at a regional freight company and does not know anyone there.

> Hi Marta, I read your piece on how the Rotterdam rail slots changed your Q3 scheduling. I run capacity plans for a 40-truck fleet and hit the same slot problem this year. One question: when you rebuilt the weekly plan, did you fix the slot first or the driver rota first? I am exploring planner roles at firms like yours, so no need to answer if you are swamped, and a pointer to someone better to ask is welcome too.

Why it works: one specific reason (her piece), one answerable question, the job interest stated lightly and without demanding anything, exit offered. Why it fails if edited badly: replace the question with "can you refer me?" and it becomes a request for a favour from a stranger.

### Worked example: freelancer to a prospective client

Situation: a freelance bookkeeper for small clinics writing to a clinic owner who recently posted about hiring a front-desk manager.

> Hello Dr Okafor, I saw you are hiring a front-desk manager, which usually means the billing backlog is part of the problem. I do month-end bookkeeping for small clinics (two dentists and a physio in your area), and the first thing I check is unmatched insurer payments. If it is useful I can send a one-page checklist of what I look at, no strings. If bookkeeping is already sorted, ignore this and good luck with the hire.

Why it works: the reason is a visible event, the offer is small and free to accept, the proof is concrete (types of clients), the exit is explicit. Do not claim results you cannot back; the owner will check the profile.

### Worked example: referral request to a second-degree contact

Situation: the person wants an introduction to a hiring manager through a mutual contact, Daniel, whom they have met once.

> Hi Daniel, we met at the nursing informatics meetup in March. I noticed you are connected to Priya Rao, who leads clinical systems at St Anne's, where I have applied for the analyst role (posted last week). Would you be comfortable forwarding her a two-line note from me, or telling me if that would be awkward? I will keep it easy for you: I can write the note and you only forward it. No problem at all if you would rather not.

Why it works: it names the exact link, the exact role, and a minimal action; it hands Daniel a way to decline without friction; it asks for forwarding, not for an endorsement of someone he barely knows. Ask for a referral only from someone who can honestly vouch for something about you; if they cannot, ask for the introduction alone.

### Restrictions

Automation and prohibited extensions risk restriction or account shutdown [documented]. Do not help the person automate sending, scrape, or use sequencing tools; advise sending a small number of individually written messages. Triggers described by tool vendors (volume, low acceptance, "I don't know this person" reports) are vendor knowledge, not documented thresholds.

LinkedIn now requires workplace verification for members adding recruiter titles [documented, March 2026], which helps the person judge whether a recruiter message is real.

## 4. Commenting

Commenting is the cheapest visible activity: no content to produce, less downside than a post, and it lands in front of an audience that someone else built. It also feeds the Activity section (section 6). Whether it produces outcomes is unmeasured.

What a substantive comment is [practice, no dataset]:
- It adds something the author would want to answer: a fact, a first-hand experience, a counterexample, or a real question.
- It stays on the post's point rather than summarising it or praising it.
- It is on a post by someone the person's target reader also reads, so the comment is seen by the right people.
- It is written by the person, in their voice, and could not be pasted under any other post.

Timing: one marketing page analysing about 657,000 comments reports that early comments earn several times the impressions of later ones [vendor: Taplio, citing LinkHub data]. That is about the comment's impressions, not profile visits, and the source sells posting tools. Direction only: do not tell people to stay glued to the feed.

What triggers limits [documented]: LinkedIn added to its help pages in 2025 that excessive comment creation or an automation tool may reduce comment visibility and may cap how many comments a member can make in a period, with no thresholds given. A 2026 LinkedIn executive interview adds that unusually fast or frequent commenting, and AI-style language, are signals [documented, executive statement, classifier not independently verified]. Practical rule: a handful of considered comments a week from a real person is nowhere near that profile. Do not suggest a quota to hit.

Do not claim that commenting beats posting for inbound. The "more inbound from comments" claims are self-reports from tool sellers and creators [contested, no independent study].

## 5. Posting

### What LinkedIn has said (2025 and 2026)

- March 2026: ranking is being rebuilt with LLM-based retrieval and generative recommenders to better understand what a post is about; signals named are profile information (industry, skills, experience, geography) and how members engage over time. LinkedIn is curbing pods, comment automation, unauthorised tools, repetitive low-substance posts, "comment to agree" prompts, and videos that do not match their text [documented]. No numbers; no statement on dwell time, saves, sends or ideal frequency.
- Peer-reviewed or preprint engineering papers describe the move to sequence-model retrieval and ranking [documented]. Practitioner claims that one named model "is" the live feed are [contested].
- May 2026: a LinkedIn executive said reach is reduced for generic AI-written posts and comments, often to first-degree connections, with no numbers and a gradual rollout [documented, executive interview].
- Relayed interviews with LinkedIn staff (secondhand): hashtags are "nice to have", links are fine if the post carries value, expertise is inferred from profile and activity, scheduling is not penalised [documented secondhand, treat lightly].

Every specific weight for dwell time, saves, sends, links, hashtags, the "golden hour" or first-comment tactics is undocumented. Describe direction only. The sequence-model design implies that the same post ranks differently for different people and that topical consistency plausibly helps [inference].

### What the independent datasets agree on, and where they conflict

Warning first: every dataset below comes from a vendor that sells posting tools, schedulers, analytics or AI detection [vendor]. Their samples are their own users' posts. Treat the results as hypotheses about the feed, not laws.

Agreement (direction):
- Organic reach per post fell sharply since 2023 [contested on magnitude]. The largest annual report (Richard van der Blom, Algorithm Insights, a consultant who sells training) reports reach down on the order of half in one year in its 2025 edition; summaries of the same report give different percentages, and the paid primary report was not read for this file.
- Documents and carousels lead on engagement rate; link posts sit at the bottom [vendor: Socialinsider 2026 benchmarks, company pages only; Buffer; AuthoredUp]. Plain text is near neutral on personal profiles.
- Personal profiles reach far more people than company pages [vendor: van der Blom; Socialinsider scope note: company-page data does not transfer to personal profiles].
- Consistent posting beats sporadic posting within the same account, with no demonstrated penalty for posting often [vendor: Buffer, 2M+ posts, within-account design, which is the strongest method of the set but still association, not causation].
- Hashtags show no reach effect and, per one vendor, many may hurt [vendor: AuthoredUp]; LinkedIn calls them optional.

Conflict (magnitude and some direction):
- Links in the post body: one report finds a median reach cut near a fifth; LinkedIn staff say links are fine; a vendor's own page contradicts itself [contested]. Safe default: put the link where the reader needs it and do not build the post around it.
- Polls and native video: rankings differ by dataset and between personal and company accounts [contested].
- Best posting time: weekday afternoons and evenings in the reader's local time look strongest in one scheduler dataset; others say there is no universal best time [contested].
- Reach decline figures and the share of the feed from connections versus interests [contested].

### Writing a post a specific reader finds useful

Use only the frameworks from paid curricula that solve a real problem, in plain words:

1. Write to one reader. Name them in your head (the clinic owner, the hiring manager at a freight firm, the junior analyst you were). A post for everyone attracts no one, and the feed is trying to match posts to people who care about the topic.
2. Keep a small set of recurring topics, two to four, tied to the person's goal and to what the profile already claims. The reader who sees three posts on one subject believes the headline more; the feed, built to infer expertise from activity, has something consistent to read [inference]. Resist topics that dilute the profile.
3. Make the first line earn the second. LinkedIn truncates a post after a few lines, so the opening must tell the right reader why to open it. Lead with the specific thing: the result, the surprise, the decision. Do not lead with a warm-up, a vague teaser or a manufactured cliffhanger. If the first line only works for people who already know who you are, rewrite it.
4. Put the useful thing in the post, not behind a click. A person who finishes the post knowing something they did not, or seeing how you think, has been given the evidence the BELIEVED gate wants.
5. End with the one next step you actually want, or with nothing. A genuine question to the reader is fine; "comment YES" is engagement bait (section 7).

Skip the rest of the course material: polarising stances for their own sake (it risks rage bait and alienating the exact reader you want), repurposing the same post repeatedly (LinkedIn is reducing recycled content), and rigid formats or daily quotas.

Cadence: the data does not require a quota. For anyone who posts, a rhythm they can sustain for months beats a burst. Buffer's range (two to five a week) is an observed ceiling for its users, not a target for a person with a day job [vendor, inference].

### Using native analytics

Population datasets conflict, so the person's own analytics are the only trustworthy tuning signal (section 8). Ask them to look at who the impressions came from (industry, seniority, location) against the intended reader before anything else. Impressions from the wrong audience are noise.

## 6. The Activity section

A visitor to the profile can see recent posts, comments and reactions in the Activity section, subject to visibility settings, and the section is hidden after 360 days with no activity [documented]. A member can control who can follow their updates and control comments per post [documented].

No study measures how it changes a visitor's impression. [practice]: a recruiter or buyer may click through to see what the person comments on, so a blank section is neutral, a section of thoughtful comments and relevant posts is a small plus, and a section of generic or off-topic reactions or a burst of one-line comments is a small minus. Practical consequences:
- What the person comments on is part of their public profile. Review the last few items before sending outreach to anyone who will look.
- If the person is job hunting while employed and cannot be visibly active, check the visibility settings first, and choose silence over odd activity.
- If the section is empty, do not fill it just to fill it. Hidden-after-360-days means nothing is the default, and nothing is fine.

## 7. Anti-patterns and their documented consequences

- Engagement pods: not allowed; LinkedIn has removed pod groups, contacted members who showed pod behaviour, warned of restrictions and removal from programmes such as Top Voices, and says it actively limits the reach of inauthentic activity [documented, March 2026]. Never recommend, describe or help set up one.
- Engagement bait ("comment YES for the link", "type 1 if you agree"): LinkedIn is reducing "comment to agree" prompts [documented, March 2026]. Also reduced: attention-bait videos and videos that do not match their text.
- Comment automation: automated comments through any browser extension, script or third-party tool are not allowed; if detected LinkedIn may hide them from the "most relevant" view and sometimes from outside the commenter's network, and may restrict the member [documented]. Detection reportedly uses speed, frequency and language [documented, executive interview].
- Automation tools generally (invitation senders, message sequencers, scrapers, browser extensions that act for you): prohibited, with restriction or shutdown as the stated risk, and tools may stop working without notice [documented]. Vendor guides describe "safe limits"; no official safe limit exists. Do not suggest limits, settings or workarounds. Offer the manual version of the task instead.
- Purchased engagement and engagement exchanges: same family as pods. Do not recommend.
- Generic machine-written posts and comments: LinkedIn's stated position is reduced reach, often to first-degree connections, for generic AI-written posts and comments, with classifiers trained on editor-labelled examples and engagement patterns [documented, May 2026 executive interview, classifier unverified]. Prevalence data from AI-detection vendors (very large shares of long posts flagged) are [vendor, contested], and detectors can misclassify non-native writers, so do not tell a person their post "reads as AI" on the basis of a detector.
- What is not targeted: editing your own substance with AI (tightening, translating, fixing grammar, restructuring your own notes) is not the stated target; publishing generic output the person did not shape is [inference from the documented language]. Separately, LinkedIn has no documented penalty for AI-assisted text in profiles. Advise: the claim, the evidence and the voice must be the person's.
- Humble-brag formulas and recycled hustle stories: no penalty documented by name; plausibly covered by the "repetitive low-substance posts" reduction [inference]. Mostly a credibility problem with the reader.

## 8. Durable versus this year's weather

Never state a right-hand item as a law. If the person asks "what does the algorithm want", answer with the left column, then say the rest is weather and point to their own analytics. Prefer the person's native analytics over any population dataset: it is their audience, it costs nothing, and the datasets conflict.

| Durable (holds across years and independent sources) | This year's weather (changes or is contested) |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn opposes pods, engagement bait and automation, and enforcement is increasing [documented] | Exact reach decline percentages (direction agreed, size disputed) |
| Moderately weak ties transmit job information best [measured] | LLM-based ranking and the shift toward interests over relationships; reduced reach for generic AI content (rollout "months" from spring 2026) |
| A warm referral beats a cold application [measured, practice] | Format rankings: polls, video, document multipliers |
| Short, specific outreach beats long [documented, dated; practice] | Link, hashtag and tagging penalty sizes |
| Closer connection degree makes a profile more likely to surface [documented] | Best days and hours to post; the "first hour" theory (undocumented) |
| Notes on invitations change replies more than acceptance [vendor, consistent direction] | Anything about dwell time, saves or sends weights (undocumented) |
| Company pages reach less than personal profiles [vendor, consistent] | Whether posting frequency has a ceiling |
| Consistency within one account beats bursts [vendor, within-account] | Invitation and comment caps (unpublished) |

## 9. Paid tiers

Prices and allowances vary by market and date: check in the app.

- Premium (Career and similar plans): adds a limited InMail allowance, the full list of who viewed your profile, applicant insights on postings, and more personalised invitation notes [documented features]. There is no independent evidence it increases interviews or hires; the reviews that exist come from career-tool vendors who commonly say free networking can match it [vendor]. Verdict: situational, never core. Worth a free trial during an active, targeted job search or when the person needs insight into who looks at their profile; otherwise skip it. Never buy it as a substitute for outreach quality.
- Sales Navigator: lead search filters, alerts and a larger InMail allowance, built for outbound sales [documented]. No study compares it with Premium on outcomes. Verdict: pay only if the person prospects weekly and has a defined buyer list; for anyone else it is unneeded. The weekly invitation figures attached to it by tool vendors are unofficial.
- Social Selling Index (SSI): a 0 to 100 score from four equally weighted elements: establish professional brand, find the right people, engage with insights, build relationships [documented]. LinkedIn's older marketing claims that higher-scoring sellers create more opportunities [vendor: LinkedIn marketing, roughly 2014 to 2015, correlational, self-selected]; its newer pages say the score does not always reflect sales efficacy or correlate with measurable outcomes [documented]. The "good" and "okay" score bands come from marketers, not LinkedIn. Verdict: a rough activity gauge for a sales team, not a goal and not something to optimise. Do not recommend raising it as an objective.
- Creator analytics (free): impressions, reactions, comments, reposts and audience demographics on one's own posts [documented]. Real data about the person's own audience, and the right place to learn. Third-party tools layer benchmarks on top; they sell posting.

## 10. Reviewing a draft the person brings

Ask these of a post, comment or message, in order. Stop and fix at the first failure.

1. Who is the one reader, and what do they need to decide? If the person cannot say, the draft is not ready.
2. Is it consistent with the profile? A reader who clicks through should find the same claim, the same specialism, the same tone. Mismatch costs belief.
3. Is there one specific thing in it that only this person could write: a number, a situation, a decision, a mistake? If the draft would survive being pasted under someone else's name, it is generic.
4. Does the first line (or the first clause of a message) tell the right reader why to continue, without a warm-up?
5. For a post: is the useful part in the post itself, not behind a link or a "comment for the guide"? For a message: is there exactly one small ask and a way out?
6. For a comment: would the author want to reply to it? Does it add rather than agree or summarise?
7. Is every claim something the person can back if the reader checks? Cut anything they cannot.
8. Could it embarrass them with a current employer, a client or a colleague? Check the situating answer on risk surface before posting about work.
9. Did a tool write most of it? If so, ask the person to rewrite the key sentence in their own words and add one detail from their own experience; do not tell them to hide it.
10. Does the ask match the relationship? A first message to a stranger asks for a sentence, not a favour.

When the draft passes, say what is working in one line and stop. Do not pad with extra suggestions; every added element is one more place for the draft to turn generic.
