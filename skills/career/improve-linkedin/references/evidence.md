# Evidence: the claim register

Read this when the person quotes a statistic or a rule they heard, when you are about to cite a number yourself, or when they ask whether a course or coach is worth paying for. It tells you how to grade a claim, what is actually known, and what to say instead of the folklore.

## 1. How to grade a claim

Grade every number or rule before you say it. If you cannot name the grade, treat it as folklore.

| Grade | Meaning | What it licenses | One plain sentence for the person |
|---|---|---|---|
| documented | LinkedIn's own help or engineering pages state it | State it as how the product works, with the date or "as of" | "LinkedIn's help page says this, as of late 2026." |
| measured | Independent or peer-reviewed data | Cite it with its limits (who was studied, what was measured) | "A study of this found X, though it only looked at Y." |
| vendor | A number from LinkedIn marketing or a company that sells something | Give the direction, attribute the number, say it is unaudited | "LinkedIn says X, but it has never shown how it counted, so take the direction, not the figure." |
| practice | Practitioners agree, no data | Say "the common advice is" and give the reasoning | "The usual advice is X, because Y, though nobody has tested it." |
| folklore | Untraceable or contradicted | Do not repeat it; if the person raises it, say where it comes from | "That number gets repeated a lot, but I can't find where it started, so I wouldn't plan around it." |

Rules of use:
- "Documented" means LinkedIn says it, not that it is true or that it matters. LinkedIn's own outcome figures are vendor grade even when they sit on a help page.
- A claim can have two grades: a documented direction and a folklore number. Grade each part separately.
- A date matters. Most LinkedIn multipliers are from 2016 to 2018. Say how old a figure is.
- Never let a grade drift upward by repetition. Ten blogs quoting one unsourced figure is still one unsourced figure.
- Advice that does not need a number does not need a grade. Do not decorate good advice with statistics.

Worked examples of the phrasing:
- Person: "I read photos get 21x more views." You: "LinkedIn published that in 2017 as an 'up to' figure with no method, so I'd take the direction, a clear photo helps, and ignore the multiplier."
- Person: "Is it true the algorithm punishes AI text?" You: "For your profile, nothing documents that. For posts, a LinkedIn executive said this year that reach is being reduced for generic AI-written posts, with no numbers. Any exact percentage you have seen is invented."
- Person: "How many hashtags?" You: "The common advice is a few or none, because LinkedIn calls them nice to have and one large dataset found no reach effect."
- You are tempted to say "recruiters decide in seconds": stop. It is folklore for LinkedIn, so say "write the first line so it works alone" and give no number.

When the person is anxious about a rule, the cheapest honest answer is often the documented one plus "unknown". Do not fill a gap with a plausible number to sound sure.

## 2. What is actually known

Short list. Each line says what it shows and what it does not.

1. Weak ties, Science 2022 (Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson, Aral). Randomised changes to People You May Know over about 20 million users and five years. Job transmission followed an inverted U: moderately weak ties (about 10 mutual connections at the peak) helped most. Does show: who you connect to causally affects job moves, more in digital industries. Does not show: that profile edits, posts or messages do anything, or that this holds outside LinkedIn's own suggestion engine. Measured, LinkedIn-run.
2. Referral studies (NY Fed staff report on one firm; NBER field experiments). Referred hires are hired more, pay a small initial premium, churn less, and referrals carry real information. Does show: a warm introduction beats a cold application. Does not show: that a LinkedIn message works like a referral. Vendor claims such as "referred candidates are 4.5x more likely to be hired" are vendor grade.
3. Sparse profiles lower hireability ratings (Journal of Business and Psychology, 2025, peer reviewed experiment). Raters judged profiles with less content as less hireable. Does show: a bare profile costs you with a human reader. Does not show: effects on real hiring outcomes, or any size of benefit from filling in more. Read from a search snippet, not the full paper.
4. Recruiter field-to-filter mapping (documented, LinkedIn help a414428). Job titles filter reads titles in Experience; Company reads Company Name; years of experience counts from the start of your earliest position; Industry is your selected Industry; the Skills filter matches the Skills section and related keywords in other sections; Locations, Schools and Languages read their own fields. Does show: which fields are structured and clickable. Does not show: how results are ranked within a filter, or how often recruiters use each filter.
5. Skills matching (documented, a793433). Explicit skills are in your Skills section; implicit skills are extracted from summary, position text, title and headline. Does show: listing a skill and using it in role text both count. Does not show: relative weight.
6. Skills cap is 100 (documented, a549047). Old guides say 50.
7. Open to Work visibility (documented, a67405). "Recruiters only" is shown to Recruiter users and LinkedIn tries to hide it from Recruiters at your current company but says it cannot guarantee that. The green frame appears only under "All LinkedIn members".
8. Pods and automation (documented, newsroom March 2026; help a1341387). Pods, automated comments and third-party automation tools are prohibited. Stated consequences: reduced reach, comments hidden from Most Relevant, restriction, removal from programs such as Top Voices. Does show: enforcement is real and growing. Does not show: any detection accuracy or safe thresholds. No official "safe limit" exists.
9. Feed stance on generic AI content (an executive interview, May 2026, no numbers). Reach is being reduced for generic AI-written posts and comments, often to first-degree connections, with gradual rollout. Does show: the direction. Does not show: how detection works in practice, accuracy, or the size of any cut.
10. Search proximity (documented, a50991). Closer connections surface more readily and LinkedIn does not guarantee an order. Does show: network distance matters in people search. Does not show: weights.
11. Verification does not make you more likely to be hired (documented, a1492056, on the jobs badge). It says nothing about search ranking.
12. Posts, large datasets (vendor, but consistent): documents and carousels lead on engagement rate, link posts sit at the bottom, company pages reach far less than personal profiles, and consistent posting beats sporadic posting within the same account. Direction is reliable, magnitudes are not.

## 3. The register

"Instead" is what you say. Grades follow section 1.

| A photo gives "21x more views, 9x more connection requests" (sometimes "36x more messages") | vendor | LinkedIn blog, 2017, "up to" maxima, no method; the 36x has no LinkedIn source | "Profiles with photos look more trustworthy and people click them more; LinkedIn's old 21x is an unaudited 'up to' figure from 2017." Direction yes, number no. |
| Five or more skills gives "17x more views" or "33x more messages" | vendor | LinkedIn blog, January 2018, correlation among active users | "Recruiters filter on skills, so list the ones you want found for. The 17x is a 2018 LinkedIn marketing number with no method." |

## 4. Why the numbers are bad

Use this to reason about a claim that is not in the register.

- LinkedIn's own multipliers (photo, skills, verification, Open to Work, Top Choice, AI search) are unaudited correlations. People who add a photo, skills or a verification are, as a group, more active users. More activity produces more views and messages. The comparison group is inactive users, so the effect of the photo itself cannot be separated. Almost all date from 2016 to 2018, when the product and the feed were different. Most say "up to", a maximum, not an average.
- The large content datasets (reach, formats, timing, frequency, links, hashtags, AI share) come from companies that sell scheduling, analytics, writing tools, outreach automation or AI detectors. Each samples its own customers, who are not typical LinkedIn users, and each has a commercial reason to find that posting more, or detecting more, matters. Two reports on the same dataset can disagree by double digits. Within-account designs (comparing an account with itself) are the strongest of these; they still show association only.
- Survey figures about recruiters, buyers, freelancers and hiring managers come mostly from career-tool and resume vendors. Questions are leading, samples are self-selected, and the quoted percentage often has no link to a method.
- Aggregator blogs launder numbers. A figure that appears on forty pages usually traces to one weak origin or to none. Search for the earliest instance; if it is a marketing post with no method, grade it vendor.
- Outcome data is almost absent. Views, impressions and reactions are easy to measure, so they get measured. Interviews, offers, clients and revenue caused by a profile edit or a post are not measured anywhere the research could find.
- The platform changes. A ranking claim from before 2025 predates LLM-based search and feed retrieval. Treat anything about "the algorithm" as weather, not law.
- Quick tests for a new claim: Is there a named method? Who sells something that depends on the claim? Is it "up to"? Is it about LinkedIn or about resumes, email or another platform? Is it older than 2025? Does it use a round multiplier (10x, 40x) with no sample size?

## 5. Are courses and coaches worth paying for?

What paid products teach, as one outline. Most single courses cover about half of this, and each is one of three products: job-seeker profile, creator or personal-brand growth, B2B lead generation.
- Profile: photo, banner, headline, About, custom URL, skills, recommendations, keywords. The profile as a landing page with a clear offer and a call to action.
- Positioning: a niche, an ideal reader or client, a one-line statement of what you do, sometimes a backstory and a strong point of view.
- Content: formats, timing, routine, hooks, storytelling, copywriting, a small set of recurring topics, repurposing, "writing for the algorithm", analytics.
- Network and outreach: finding relevant people, engaging with their posts, connecting, direct messages, selling.
- Proof: client results and case studies as content.
- Job search (some): finding roles, salary negotiation. Company pages (a few). AI helpers, templates and checklists (newer).
- Rarely covered: how to read your own analytics, company pages, outreach to hiring managers.

What the money buys that free knowledge does not:
- A personal review of your actual profile and drafts by a competent person. This is the main real asset, because generic advice cannot diagnose your positioning.
- Accountability and deadlines, such as a live cohort that makes you ship several posts. The value depends on whether you will use it.
- Community or peers who respond early. Real but gameable, and less useful as the feed down-weights coordinated engagement.
- Done-for-you writing, which is labour, not knowledge.

What is packaging:
- Branded frameworks (a content matrix, a profile funnel, a backstory builder), mostly familiar ideas relabelled.
- Templates and swipe files. Convenient, and nobody's moat.
- Badges and certificates, which are signals, not content.
- "Proven by my results" claims. Survivorship, not evidence; none of the sales pages the research read gave verifiable outcomes.
- Curation: a 90-minute course saves search time. Real, but small.

Price bands seen, with no names: short self-paced creator courses about 80 to 120 dollars; marketplace courses about 15 to 50 dollars on sale; platform subscriptions about 240 dollars a year; live cohorts and coaching programmes roughly 700 to 1,200 dollars or more; community memberships about 1,200 dollars a year (unverified); profile rewrites about 50 to 400 dollars on freelance marketplaces, 90 dollars to 1,500 dollars through resume and career firms. Free alternatives cover the core: LinkedIn's own help and Learning library, free language-specific courses, and creators' free posts.

Honest answer to give: do not pay for information. The core (profile basics, a clear audience, useful posts, real engagement with others) is free, and tactics in courses recorded before about 2025 may be stale. If you pay for anything, pay for a human review of your profile and first posts, or for a short cohort if you know you will not ship without a deadline, and prefer refundable terms. Be wary of any promise of a result, since none has been audited. Limit of this research: it could read sales pages, syllabi, listings and reviews, but not the paid content itself, so it cannot say whether a given course has deeper material than its marketing.

## 6. What nobody has measured

Say "unknown" for these.
- How long recruiters spend on a LinkedIn profile, or which section they read first. The only eye-tracking study is from 2012 (30 recruiters, vendor-funded, unclear whether it covered resumes or profiles).
- The independent effect of a photo, skill count, headline, About text, location or banner on views or messages.
- Ranking weights for any profile field, or how Recruiter ranks within a filter. Which profile fields feed LinkedIn's LLM-based people-search summaries is unpublished.
- Whether endorsements, completeness, verification or Open to Work affect ranking.
- Whether the public Open to Work frame helps or hurts, compared with "recruiters only".
- Outcomes of posting for job seekers (interviews, offers), or of Activity-section impressions.
- How long any goal takes: job search, pivot, freelance pipeline, thought leadership.
- Whether editing a post affects reach, and the real size of any link penalty.
- Weights for dwell time, saves, sends, comments versus reactions.
- Detection accuracy and reach cut for generic AI content in the feed.
- Official weekly invitation limits and current note allowances.
- Recruiter reactions to machine-translated or second-language profiles, and visibility gain from a second language.
- Anything on hospitality, retail, teaching, trades and nonprofit LinkedIn outcomes.
- Whether a paid course or coach produces better outcomes than free material (no controlled comparison exists).
