# Languages and markets

Read this when the person is not writing in English for a US reader, works across borders, targets a specific country, or asks which language to use. It holds the platform mechanics for multilingual profiles, a way to choose languages, and what is and is not known about each market.

Facts as of November 2026; anything marked (check live) changes often. Untagged guidance is [practice]. Tags: [documented], [measured], [vendor], [practice], [contested], [inference], [weak].

## 1. What LinkedIn documents about language versions

- A secondary-language profile is created on desktop only, not in the mobile app: Me, profile card, edit icon beside "Profile language", "Add language" [documented]. (check live)
- Nothing is translated for you. Only first name, last name (if they differ in that language) and headline are prompted; every other field you must write yourself [documented].
- Any language can be toggled as primary. No limit on the number of versions is stated [documented].
- A signed-in viewer sees the version matching their interface language; if none matches, they see the primary version (the language the account was created in). Viewers can switch manually; signed-out viewers get a "View this profile in another language" dropdown [documented].
- Each version has its own URL and appears in search engine results [documented]. Practitioners say each URL can be customised, for example with an "-en" suffix [practice].
- Consequence: the version a recruiter sees follows their interface language, not their country and not the language of their search. A recruiter in Brazil whose LinkedIn is set to English sees your English version [inference].
- Not documented: how LinkedIn member search or Recruiter indexes each version, whether versions are merged, whether a keyword in one version is found by a query in another. LinkedIn says extra languages "can help members and recruiters find you more easily" and nothing more [documented]. Any percentage uplift or specific ranking rule for second-language profiles is unverified; do not repeat one.
- Safe working rule: assume text is matched in the language it is written in. Put the terms the reader types in the language of the version that reader will see, and never rely on a keyword in one version to surface you in the other [inference].
- Name fields: the secondary-language profile allows a different first and last name per language, which is the supported way to show native script next to a Latin spelling [documented]. Name pronunciation recordings exist, recorded in the mobile app [documented]. LinkedIn does not document how search normalises name variants, so keep the passport or work-document form as the main one [practice].
- Interface languages: 25 by 2021 and 36 after ten were added in September 2024 [measured, press report]. Whether Brazilian and European Portuguese are separate interface options is unverified; check the language picker live before promising a PT-BR and a PT-PT version.
- Posts are not translated automatically; a reader taps "See translation" (Microsoft machine translation, 60+ languages) [documented]. Consequence: a post's language picks its audience. Write in the language of the readers you want, not the one you are most fluent in [inference].

## 2. Choosing the language: a procedure by reader

Start from the reader, not the person. Ask: who must act on this profile (recruiter, hiring manager, client, investor), what language do they use LinkedIn in, and which words do they type into search? The answers decide everything below. If the person does not know, find out from ads, from who has contacted them, from the people already doing the target job in that market.

1. Pick the primary language as the one the target reader's interface is most likely set to and the one the search terms are in. A Brazilian targeting Brazilian employers: Portuguese. The same person targeting foreign employers or a multinational that works in English: English. Primary matters because it is the fallback for any viewer whose interface matches no version [documented].
2. Add a second version when two distinct reader groups exist and the person can write both well. Typical case: local employers and international ones. Write the second version in full (summary, experience, skills), not only the headline [documented: only name and headline are prompted, which is why people stop there].
3. Use one profile with mixed vocabulary when the field's working language is English-heavy and the prose is local. Example: Brazilian software, product and marketing, where ads say "Machine Learning Engineer", "roadmap", "stakeholder" inside Portuguese sentences [practice]. Keep the prose in one language and the field terms in the form the ads use.
4. Do not hedge inside a block (half Portuguese, half English in the same paragraph). Neither reader gets a coherent read. Two versions beat one blended one [practice].
5. Treat a second-language profile as a liability when the person could not defend it in an interview in that language. A recruiter who opens the English version will often reply in English. Profile fluency that the person cannot match live is a credibility problem, not a visibility gain [practice]. If the person's English is functional but weak, a plainly written, accurate English profile beats an ornate one, and the headline should not claim "fluent" or "bilingual" beyond what they can show.
6. For a person abroad or relocating, the reader is in the new country: use that country's language if the person works in it, English if the employer does. Do not keep the old country's language as primary out of habit.
7. For posts, choose per post by audience. One language per post [practice]. If the person has two audiences, post in each language separately rather than translating line by line.
8. Recommendations in the target language help foreign readers who do not know local employers [weak]; ask for one or two when the person is crossing language lines.

Evidence note: all language-strategy guidance in this section comes from recruiters, CV vendors and language schools. No independent study measures its effect [practice].

## 3. Keywords, titles, degrees and names across languages

Keywords
- Harvest terms from 5 to 10 real job ads in the target market and language, for the exact role. Count which title, which tools and which verbs repeat, and use those forms. This beats any translation or keyword list the advisor could produce from memory [practice].
- Avoid stuffing a skills list with synonyms; it reads as machine-made and the headline carries most of the weight [practice].
- The headline is the most-translated field and the one LinkedIn prompts you to write per language [documented]. Put the searchable title there.

Titles
- Many titles do not map one to one: Brazilian "Analista Pleno/Sênior", "Coordenador", "Gerente"; German "Geschäftsführer" against CEO or Managing Director; French "cadre"; Japanese "bucho" against Director or VP. Do not trust a translation table. Describe scope instead: team size, budget, reporting line, systems or customers owned [practice].
- Fields where ads use English titles (software, data, product, finance in many markets): use the English title even on a local-language profile [practice].
- Fields with strong local titles or licensing (law, public sector, accounting, healthcare, teaching, regulated engineering such as German "Ingenieur"): keep the local title and add a short English gloss in the headline or title line, with scope [practice].
- If the ads for the target role use a title the person has never held, do not adopt it. Use their real title and put the ad's vocabulary in the description of the work.

Degrees
- Degrees do not map one to one. A Brazilian "MBA" is usually a lato sensu post-graduate course, not a US-style research or full-time MBA; "Pós-graduação lato sensu" is not a Master's, and "Mestrado" (stricto sensu) is [practice]. Write the real name, the institution and a plain gloss ("postgraduate specialisation, 360 hours") rather than "MBA" alone if the reader is foreign. Never upgrade the label.
- Honorific prefixes (Dr., Prof., Dipl.-Ing., Mag., Ing., Lic., Dott.) are normal on German-speaking and some Southern European profiles and absent on US ones. Put the credential where local readers expect it, and keep the name field itself clean where possible [practice].

Names and scripts
- Main name form: the one on the person's work documents, so recruiters and background checks match [practice]. For a Japanese or Chinese name, give the native script in the matching secondary-language version and the Latin form in the English one [documented mechanism, practice convention].
- Do not translate or anglicise a name to be easier to read unless the person already uses that form professionally.

## 4. Writing natively in the target language and variant

Instruction to the advisor: write profile text directly in the target language and variant, as a native professional in that field would write it. Do not translate English phrasing. If the person supplies English and asks for a translation, rebuild each sentence from the claim it makes, not from its words, and show the person the native version with a plain-language back-translation so they can check it.

- Calques and idioms do not travel. "Move the needle", "hit the ground running", "wear many hats", "passionate about", "thought leader" read as foreign or empty in most languages. Replace with the concrete fact. The same holds in reverse for local idioms in English [practice].
- Machine translation output reads as artificial and recruiters are said to notice it [practice]. The direction is uncontroversial, though no one has measured how recruiters score it. Do not hand the person raw machine output, and do not produce text that is secretly a translation either.
- Loanwords differ by field. Brazilian tech and marketing keep sprint, stakeholder, feedback, roadmap, startup. German consulting and tech use heavy Denglisch (Meeting, Feedback, Know-how), while formal roles use German terms. French institutional usage pushes French equivalents, and Quebec is more purist than France. Match what the target ads and peers in that field use [practice].
- Regional variants are separate targets:
  - PT-BR against PT-PT: spelling (for example "acadêmica" against "académica"), vocabulary ("habilidades" in Brazil, "competências" in Portugal), and progressive forms (gerund in Brazil, "a + infinitive" in Portugal). A Brazilian applying in Portugal adapts to PT-PT or writes in English, and the reverse [practice, from commercial CV guides].
  - Spanish: Latin American against Iberian vocabulary ("computadora" against "ordenador", "celular" against "móvil"); within Latin America, country differences. Avoid voseo and slang in profile text.
  - German: Swiss German writes "ss" for "ß"; Austrian titles and terms differ.
  - English: match UK or US spelling and vocabulary to the market, and keep it consistent within a version.
- Record the variant in the working notes for the person before drafting; ask if unsure which one the target reader uses.

## 5. Markets where another channel matters

Member counts are registered accounts or ad-audience estimates and differ by 20 to 30 percent between sources; never read them as active users [measured, third-party estimates]. Ask what the specific reader uses before assuming LinkedIn is the channel.

DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) and XING
- LinkedIn and XING both have large DACH memberships, with LinkedIn ahead on size per third-party estimates [measured, Statista and NapoleonCat]. XING's role is regionally and sector-specific [contested].
- Practitioner view: XING is stronger for SMEs, trades and some German-language mid-level hiring; LinkedIn for international employers, English-language roles, tech and senior searches [practice]. Specific response-rate claims for XING are unverified; do not repeat them.
- Advice: for a German-language employer, keep a current XING profile in addition to LinkedIn; for an international employer based in the region, LinkedIn is usually the main channel. LinkedIn is for visibility to recruiters and international employers; it is not proof that the local reader is on it.

Japan
- Domestic mid-career hiring runs mostly through BizReach (scouting), Wantedly (startups, culture-first), domestic agencies and job boards. LinkedIn is used mainly by foreign-affiliated firms, global tech and bilingual recruiters [practice]. LinkedIn passed 3 million Japanese members in 2022 [vendor, press report]; later higher claims are unverified.
- Advice: a person aiming at Japanese firms needs a Japanese-language résumé and BizReach, with LinkedIn as secondary. A person aiming at foreign firms in Japan or at employers abroad should lead with LinkedIn, native-script name on a Japanese version and Latin on the English one. Language-level signals (for example JLPT level) are suggested by guides [weak].
- Keigo is expected in Japanese-language outreach; English outreach to foreign-firm recruiters carries no such expectation [practice].

China
- LinkedIn closed InCareer, its China-only jobs app, in August 2023 and ended local jobs; LinkedIn China now serves Chinese companies going abroad [measured, news report].
- Domestic hiring runs on local platforms: Maimai, Liepin, Boss Zhipin, 51job. No 2025 or 2026 usage data for them was found.
- A mainland member can hold a global LinkedIn account. LinkedIn is for reaching overseas employers and international networks, not domestic ones [inference]. Do not recommend it as a domestic jobs channel.

India
- Naukri is the volume leader for CV database search; LinkedIn is positioned for senior, passive and international hiring [practice, from vendor comparisons]. The jobseeker counts claimed by Naukri and rivals are unverified.
- English dominates professional LinkedIn use in India; the interface also offers Hindi and several regional languages [documented, 2021 launch plus additions]. Advice: a person job-hunting domestically should keep Naukri current as well; LinkedIn matters most for senior, product, tech and overseas roles.

Gulf
- Regional boards (Bayt, GulfTalent) exist alongside LinkedIn [practice]. Recruiter-usage percentages circulating for Saudi Arabia and the UAE come from CV vendors without sources; ignore them.
- Photo and presentation norms are disputed by local experts [contested]; ask the person and follow what peers in their target employer show.

Brazil and Latin America
- LinkedIn is a major channel for sourcing and visibility; large Brazilian employers often run applications through ATS portals such as Gupy while recruiters source on LinkedIn [weak]. No usable data was found on Gupy, Vagas.com, Catho, InfoJobs, Computrabajo or Bumeran. See section 7.

Not covered by the research: Nordics, UK specifics, Korea (Saramin, JobKorea), France beyond a few guide pages, Southeast Asia, Russia, Africa, the Middle East beyond the Gulf, and the share of local ATS portals anywhere. For these, say plainly that you have no market-specific evidence, give the generic gates, and have the person check ads and peers in their own market. Do not improvise authority.

## 6. Cultural norms: weak priors and what survives them

The research found almost no measured evidence for claims of the form "culture X dislikes self-promotion". The measured work is small: a corpus of about 90 French and US LinkedIn summaries suggesting French summaries signal competence more indirectly [measured, small, language-pragmatics, abstract-level]; a study of Vietnamese and US students finding that self-construal, not country, moderated discomfort with self-presentation [measured, narrow]; and an analysis of 262 Iranian accounts showing self-praise is not an American monopoly [measured, narrow]. None measures hiring outcomes.

How to use this:
- You may mention a cultural norm as a weak prior, labelled as such ("some practitioners say German-language readers prefer restraint; this is not measured"). Never present it as a rule, never use it to override what the person's actual reader and peers do, and never stereotype the person. Industry, seniority and employer shape behaviour more than nationality [weak].
- The best check is observable: look at 5 to 10 profiles of people in the target role at the target employers and note how they write.

Guidance that survives the uncertainty:
- Use concrete, checkable results in place of adjectives ("world-class", "passionate"). This reads acceptably everywhere and is the part of self-presentation that travels best [inference].
- Write "I led X within a team of N, result Y". It names the person's own contribution for readers who expect individual attribution and the team for readers who expect collective credit.
- Formal address first in languages with a formal register (Sie in German, vous in French, usted in Spanish, o senhor or a senhora in Portuguese for clearly senior or older recipients), then mirror the reply. German-speaking tech and startup users often use du, but cold first contact is safer in the formal register [practice]. In Brazil "você" is the neutral default [practice].
- Directness: a cold message with one personal reason for contact, one specific ask and an easy opt-out works in all the markets discussed. Abrupt asks can read badly where a relationship-building opener is expected [weak].
- Photo: expected on LinkedIn almost everywhere. Photos on CVs are a separate legal and cultural question in the US, UK and Germany [practice]. LinkedIn's own old statistic on photo views is dated and vendor-run [vendor].
- Omit age, birth date, marital status, children, religion, health and politics everywhere. These expose the person to bias, are legally restricted in hiring in several jurisdictions (for example under German anti-discrimination law), and are not needed by any reader [practice]. Some CV traditions still list them; do not carry them onto LinkedIn.
- Emoji and tone: said to be common in Brazil and India, sparser in DACH, the Nordics and Japan [weak]. Match the target audience's observable practice.

## 7. Brazil

Context
- LinkedIn reports 100 million Brazilian members (June 2026), third largest market after the US and India, with 11 million original feed posts and 400 million engagement actions in a quarter [documented]. Brazil has about 108 million workers, so the count includes inactive, student and non-resident accounts; do not read it as "almost every worker is active" [inference]. Third-party snapshots give lower figures [measured, differs by source].
- Posting in Portuguese reaches a large audience; Portuguese-interface viewers see the Portuguese version by default [documented, inference for the default].

Primary language by target employer (all of this is [vendor] guidance from Deel, Berlitz and Nomad Global, plus [inference] from the documented mechanics; none of it measured)
- Brazilian employers: Portuguese primary, with English field terms where ads use them.
- Multinationals and foreign employers: English primary, Portuguese secondary. A Brazilian recruiter whose interface is in Portuguese still gets the Portuguese version [inference].
- Person living in Brazil and working for non-Brazilian clients: English primary, Portuguese secondary.
- Each version has its own URL [documented]: put the English URL on CVs and applications to foreign companies and the Portuguese one on Brazilian ones.
- Write the English version in real, careful English. The writing of the profile is itself the evidence of English ability that foreign readers look for [practice]. If the person cannot interview in English, either skip the English version or keep it short and accurate and say so honestly in the language line.

What foreign employers hiring Brazilians reportedly look for [weak, from job-seeker guides; no recruiter-side survey was found]
- Demonstrated English: profile writing, English recommendations, spoken confirmation.
- Time-zone overlap and remote-work signals.
- Fit with US or EU stacks and business contexts.
- Clear explanation of Brazilian employers and degrees the reader does not know: one line on what the company does and its size, and a gloss on the degree.
- Contract structure (PJ, company registration) is discussed in guides on international remote work [weak]; leave it off the profile unless the person's target reader asks.

Titles and degrees
- "Gerente" can sit above or below a US "Manager" depending on company; "Coordenador" and "Analista Pleno/Sênior" have no clean equivalent [practice]. Describe scope in English: team size, budget, systems, customers.
- Tech titles are usually English already; non-tech titles are usually Portuguese [practice].
- Lato sensu MBA and other "pós-graduação" courses: write the real name and a plain gloss. A Brazilian MBA is not a US MBA; do not label it as one without the gloss. "Graduação" is the bachelor level, "Mestrado" a Master's [practice].

Tone
- Brazilian LinkedIn is described as warm, storytelling-oriented and emoji-friendly compared with DACH or Nordic LinkedIn [weak]. It can read as unserious to some international hiring managers [weak]. For an English profile aimed abroad, cut emotive phrasing and lean on concrete results. For a Portuguese profile aimed at Brazilian employers, keep the register natural and don't flatten it to a translated-from-English tone.
- Use native plain words rather than literal carry-overs from English business idiom. Prefer the word a Brazilian professional in the field would actually say.
- Older CV conventions (photo, age, marital status, children) are fading but still seen. Omit them on LinkedIn, especially on a profile aimed abroad [practice].

## 8. When to ask a native speaker or the person

- Before delivering text in any language the advisor cannot be confident writing natively at the level of a professional in that field, say so and ask for a native reader. This applies in particular to Japanese keigo, formal German or French outreach, and fine regional variants.
- Ask the person to check any profile text they will defend in an interview: every claim, every term, every level of language skill. If they cannot explain a sentence in that language, rewrite it or cut it.
- Ask the person to confirm: which variant (PT-BR or PT-PT, Latin American or Iberian Spanish, Swiss or German German, UK or US English), which language the target reader's interface is probably in, and whether the local title and degree translations are accurate.
- Where the research was thin (markets in the "not covered" list in section 5), tell the person what is unknown and have them check ads, peers and a local professional before relying on a rule.
- When the person disagrees with a cultural prior, defer to them. They know their field and reader better than any generalisation.
