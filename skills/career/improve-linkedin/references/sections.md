# Section-by-section craft

Read this when reviewing or rewriting a specific part of a profile. It gives, per section, the reader's question, the gate it serves, the principle, the failure modes and a test you can run on the person's actual text. It ends with three cross-cutting parts: evidence without numbers, generic text, and the honesty ceiling.

Facts as of November 2026; anything marked (check live) changes often.

Tag note: the craft advice below is [practice] (practitioners agree, little data) unless a line carries another tag. Tags used: [documented], [measured], [vendor], [practice], [contested], [inference]. There is no independent measurement isolating the effect of any single section on views or messages, so treat every "do X to get Y" as reasoning, not proof.

## How the sections divide the work

Structured fields are what search filters read; free text is what a human or a semantic matcher reads. In Recruiter, job title, company name, dates, location, industry, schools and languages are filter fields, and the Skills filter matches the Skills section plus keywords found in other sections [documented]. Headline and About are reachable through keyword search and semantic matching, not through a dedicated filter [inference]. So: get structured fields exactly right first, then spend craft on the free text a person will read. When a person asks "which section matters most", answer by gate: Headline, Skills and Experience titles serve FOUND; photo, headline and the About opening serve RECOGNISED; Experience, Recommendations and credentials serve BELIEVED; Featured, contact info, Services and the last line of About serve ACTED ON.

Suggested order of work when the person wants a full rewrite: structured fields (titles, dates, employers, location, industry, credentials), then Experience evidence, then headline, then About (it reuses the proof found in Experience), then Skills tied to roles, then Featured and contact route, then photo and banner last. Writing About first tends to produce adjectives, because the proof has not been gathered yet.

Consistency check across sections: the headline's noun should appear in the current Experience title or scope line, every top skill should be visible in some role, and the About's claims should be provable from Experience or Featured. A mismatch is a BELIEVED-gate failure even when each section is fine alone.

## 1. Photo

- Reader's question: is this a real, reachable person, and would I recognise them from this picture?
- Gate: RECOGNISED (also BELIEVED: a real face is a trust signal).
- Principle: the photo is an identity and trust signal, not a portrait contest. LinkedIn's own guidance is a recent picture that looks like you, face filling roughly 60% of the frame, dressed as for work [documented]. The direction (a clear face beats no photo or a logo) is [practice]; do not quote any "N times more views" multiplier, they trace to LinkedIn marketing with no method [vendor]. Dress and setting should match the reader's expectation of your field: scrubs suit bedside roles but may undercut a move into management; a hard hat suits site work; a suit may read oddly for a community arts worker.
- Fails when: cropped from a group shot, sunglasses, distance shot, logo or pet instead of a face, heavy filter or AI-generated face, a photo old enough that a meeting would not recognise you, a costume mismatched to the target reader.
- Test: shrink to about 40 pixels. Is there one face, eyes visible, no other person? Would a colleague pick it out? Does it look like the person today?

## 2. Banner

- Reader's question: what does this person do, at a glance, and is there a way in?
- Gate: RECOGNISED, weakly. Optional.
- Principle: the banner is free context space, not a requirement. Recommended size is 1584 x 396 pixels [documented]. Phones crop it and the profile photo covers the lower left, so keep any text near the centre and clear of the photo [practice]. Recruiter users may never see the banner in their list or profile view [practice, single source]. Do not spend long here; there is no evidence that a blank banner costs opportunities.
- Fails when: a stock quote, tiny text, text hidden behind the photo, a busy image, a claim that contradicts the profile.
- Test: view on a phone. Is any text readable and uncovered? Does it say something the headline does not (your practice, your sector, how to book you)? If not, a plain on-brand image is fine.

## 3. Name and pronunciation

- Reader's question: how do I find this person and say their name?
- Gate: FOUND (people searching by name) and ACTED ON (a correctly said name in a call or introduction).
- Principle: LinkedIn's help says the name fields should hold only first and last name, no extra information, and treats keyword stuffing as a violation [documented]. Use the name your contacts will search. An additional-name field exists for alternate spellings or nicknames (check live). Recording the pronunciation is recommended by LinkedIn's own guidance [documented]; limits on length and device (check live). Worth it for any name a stranger might mislabel, in any country.
- Fails when: keywords, emoji or job descriptors in the name field; a different name from the one on business cards and email.
- Test: would a stranger who has only the person's name from a card or a meeting find the right profile in two search words?
- Contested: credentials in the name field (RN, MD, Esq, CPA). For: a field convention in healthcare and law, shown everywhere including search lists. Against: LinkedIn's help says name fields carry only the name [documented]. Default: put credentials in the licences and certifications section (structured, verifiable) and in the headline if they define the role; use the name field only if the person insists and understands the policy risk.

## 4. Headline

- Reader's question: in a few words, what is this person's job and for whom, in words I would type into a search box?
- Gate: FOUND and RECOGNISED. The headline travels everywhere: search lists, comments, invitations, messages.
- Principle: two jobs, be findable and be chosen. LinkedIn says standard job titles are easier to find than unusual ones [documented], and Recruiter retrieval expands exact title matches to similar titles [documented, 2019]. Recruiter's job title filter reads Experience entries, not the headline [documented], so the headline wins on keyword and semantic matches and on human judgment, not on a filter. Limit is 220 characters [practice]; only the first 60 to 80 show in many lists (check live, estimates vary). Lead with the conventional noun, put the differentiator second. Never claim a title the person does not hold; for a career changer show the direction honestly ("Registered Nurse, ICU | moving into clinical informatics").
- Fails when: "Seeking opportunities" alone; invented titles nobody searches; adjectives; pipe-separated skill lists; emoji rows; a headline that contradicts Experience.
- Test: show only the first 60 characters, name covered. Could a hirer type those words into a search box and expect people like this? Count the adjectives; the target is zero.
- Contested: title only versus value proposition. One recruiter camp says hirers search plain role names, not slogans; most coaches say sell value. Default: conventional title first, value after it, so both readers are served.

## 5. About

- Reader's question: past the dates, what kind of professional is this, what are they known for, and what should I contact them about?
- Gate: RECOGNISED (opening), BELIEVED (middle), ACTED ON (last line).
- Principle: the only free-form section where the person chooses emphasis. It turns a job list into a direction. Limit 2,600 characters [practice]; the preview before "see more" is roughly 200 characters, shorter on mobile (check live, sources disagree). Write the first two sentences so they stand alone: what the person does, for whom, in plain words. Middle: two or three concrete proofs. End: how to engage (what to contact them about, where). If the person is a job seeker, say the target role and the proof for it; a plea alone says nothing.
- Fails when: opens with "passionate" or a life story before the point; copies the resume summary; is a keyword list; would be true of anyone in the field; has no route to contact.
- Test: do the first two sentences contain the words for the work the person wants to be found for, and does the section end by saying what to contact them about? This checks what is there. It is not a grade of the writing, and you never tell someone their About is generic.
- Contested: first versus third person. Third person persists for executives, lawyers and speakers whose About doubles as an external bio; most advice favours first person as warmer and native to the platform. Default: first person; third person only when the text really serves as a press bio, and then without adjectives like "visionary".
- Contested (mild): bullets versus paragraphs. Default: whichever makes the first lines most scannable; keep symbols and emoji sparse.

## 6. Featured

- Reader's question: show me the best proof you can, in one click.
- Gate: BELIEVED and ACTED ON.
- Principle: a proof shelf. Featured holds your own posts or articles, external links, and uploaded images, documents, presentations or videos; you need at least two items before you can reorder them, removal cannot be undone, and posts from Events or Groups cannot be featured [documented]. Most professions have a portable proof object: a portfolio, a published piece, a talk, a sanitised lesson sample, a case summary, a certificate page, a project write-up. Lead with the two or three that best back the headline. If nothing shareable exists, leave it empty; a random viral post is worse than nothing. Sources conflict on whether Creator Mode still gates its position on the page (check live). Do not quote any view-lift figure for Featured; none is traceable.
- Fails when: stale items, off-topic posts, broken links, an item with no title or a blank thumbnail, anything exposing client, patient or student information.
- Test: for each item, does it carry a human title and one line saying what it proves? Open each link logged out; does it work?

## 7. Experience

- Reader's question: is this history coherent, recent and relevant, and what did this person own and change?
- Gate: BELIEVED (also FOUND, through structured fields).
- Principle: the spine of the profile and the most cross-checked section. Mechanics that constrain it: Recruiter's years-of-experience is computed from the start date of the earliest position, the title filter reads titles here, the company filter reads the company name field, and seniority and function are derived from titles [documented]. So keep the earliest date present and true, use the market name of the job (formal title in brackets if different), and name the employer exactly. Skills can be tied to individual roles [documented]; do it for the skills the person most wants to be found for. Recruiter AI-assisted search and Hiring Assistant extract qualifications from profiles and show which were found against a brief [documented]; write each capability as a checkable claim with context (scale, constraint, outcome) because that is the form such systems can pick up [inference]. Description limit 2,000 characters [practice]. Per role: one line of scope (who, how many, where, with what), then two to five outcomes. Duties describe the job; the reader already knows what a nurse or teacher does. Show what this person did differently.
- Fails when: a pasted job description; every bullet opens with the same verb; superlatives with no referent; unexplained gaps or overlaps; volunteering or part-time mixed with full-time unlabelled; passive voice that hides who acted; a title or date that differs from the resume.
- Test: for each role, can a stranger answer "what changed because this person was here, and how big was it" in ten seconds? If neither, it is a duty list; go to the evidence section below.
- Contested: bullets versus short paragraph then bullets. Default: either, provided line one carries the point. LinkedIn reads more narrative than a resume, so a one-sentence scope line is worth keeping.

## 8. Skills

- Reader's question: does this person have the capabilities I am filtering for, and is there evidence?
- Gate: FOUND (primarily), BELIEVED (when tied to roles).
- Principle: the cap is 100 skills, with up to five shown as top skills, and skills can be linked to the roles where they were used [documented]. The Recruiter skills filter matches the Skills section and keywords in other sections; LinkedIn also derives implicit skills from summary, position text, title and headline, which the member cannot edit [documented]. So a skill named in a role description counts even if not listed, and a listed skill with no role behind it is a bare claim. Choose the skills a hirer would literally filter on, in the vocabulary of the field (tools, methods, standards, licences, populations). Put the three to five that name the trade as top skills. A tighter list of 10 to 25 real skills reads better than 100 undifferentiated ones [practice]; the cap is a ceiling, not a target. LinkedIn skill assessments are no longer offered [documented], so do not advise taking them. Do not quote any skills-count multiplier.
- Fails when: generic entries ("Microsoft Office") pinned as top skills, skills copied from a job advert, skills the person cannot discuss, no role links, stale skills from a past career.
- Test: for each top skill, can the person name the Experience entry that shows it in use and someone who could vouch? If not, add the evidence or drop the skill.
- Contested: endorsements. LinkedIn says they help Recruiter search; many practitioners see them as cheap and reciprocal. Default: ask a few informed people to endorse only the top skills; never chase counts or trade them.

## 9. Recommendations

- Reader's question: has anyone who worked with this person said, specifically, what it was like?
- Gate: BELIEVED.
- Principle: third-party testimony whose weight comes entirely from who wrote it and how specific it is. Few, from people who saw the work (manager, client, peer, supervisor), beat many vague ones. Advise asking soon after a visible success, giving the writer the context and what you hope they can speak to, and personalising each request. A useful one states the relationship and duration, a specific situation, what the writer saw the person do, and what it meant for them. Many arriving in the same week reads as solicited [practice]. Some regulated employers restrict recommendations (check policy).
- Fails when: all from one employer or one level, all peers or none from a client, "great to work with", obviously swapped, writers with no real profile.
- Test: pick one recommendation. Does it contain a situation and an outcome a stranger could repeat? Could it be about anyone? If so, ask the writer for a specific story, not more praise.

## 10. Education, licences and certifications

- Reader's question: what here can I verify, and is it current?
- Gate: BELIEVED, and FOUND for regulated roles (schools, degrees, fields and graduation year are filters in Recruiter [documented]).
- Principle: put anything an issuer can confirm into the structured fields (licence or certificate name, issuer, dates, credential ID, URL where it exists) rather than only into headline text, so it is searchable and checkable [inference]. In regulated work (nursing, trades, law, teaching) state licence type, jurisdiction and status; leave the licence number off unless the board permits publication. Education dates are optional later in a career, which also limits age-based filtering; but the earliest Experience start date drives years of experience, so do not remove that. A sparse or incomplete profile lowers hireability ratings in an independent experiment [measured, raters not real hires], so complete the structured sections that apply. Unfinished degrees say "attended" or the programme completed, never imply the degree. Identity and workplace verification badges exist; do them for trust, not for expected ranking (LinkedIn does not claim they raise hiring odds on jobs [documented]).
- Fails when: expired credentials shown as current; a course certificate presented as a qualification; every webinar listed; a licence number the board forbids publishing.
- Test: can a third party confirm each credential at the issuer in under two minutes?
- Contested: omitting education dates. For: reduces age bias. Against: some recruiters read missing dates as hiding something. Default: omit them after roughly 15 years of experience, never hide the degree itself.

## 11. Projects, publications, volunteering, languages

- Reader's question: is there extra evidence of skill or relevance that the job list does not show?
- Gate: BELIEVED, plus FOUND through the language filters.
- Principle: include only items that add evidence for the target reader. Volunteering counts as experience when it used a real skill; label it as volunteering and give it dates. Spoken languages and the profile language are separate Recruiter filters [documented]: list spoken languages with level where the role serves multilingual people (care, teaching, law, hospitality, public service), and note that the reader's market language may differ from the profile language (a second-language profile section can serve a second market).
- Fails when: a dump of everything ever done, unlabelled volunteering mixed with paid work, a language claimed above real level, a project with no link or outcome.
- Test: for each item, which reader decision does it help? If none, cut it.

## 12. Custom URL and contact info

- Reader's question: can I remember, share and reach this person?
- Gate: ACTED ON (also FOUND by name).
- Principle: the custom URL is 3 to 100 characters, no spaces or symbols, cannot contain "LinkedIn", is first come first served, one at a time, with up to five changes in six months [documented]. Use a version of the name; add a disambiguator (field, initial, city) only when the name is taken. Change it once and early: it appears on cards, signatures and CVs. Contact info: publish what the reader will use (email for clients, portfolio or practice site for creative and trade work). Weigh exposure: a personal phone or home address is rarely needed, and some boards restrict what regulated professionals publish.
- Fails when: a string of digits, a former employer in the URL, no way to be reached for someone who wants inbound work, personal data published by default.
- Test: if the person's profile were printed on a card, would the reader reach them in one step?

## 13. Services

- Reader's question: can I hire this person for a specific problem, and where?
- Gate: ACTED ON.
- Principle: Service Pages are free landing pages for freelancers and small businesses; media and a "Request proposal" button need Premium Business, Sales Navigator or Recruiter Lite [documented]. Worth it for solo practitioners (trades, consultants, coaches, small law or accounting practices) who want enquiries. Describe the client's problem and the area served, not a list of service names. Not worth it for a salaried person who wants a job; it signals a side business to an employer.
- Fails when: a bare service-name list, no location, no way to reply, a page that contradicts the headline.
- Test: could a stranger with the problem tell, in two lines, that this is for them and what to do next?

## 14. Evidence when the job has no obvious numbers

Principle: a number is only one kind of evidence. A claim a stranger can check or picture is weighty; an adjective is not. Never invent a figure: an estimate must be labelled as one, and the person must be able to defend it in an interview. Ask the person these questions, in this order, and write only what they answer.

Alternatives to quantification:
- Scope: headcount, patients, pupils, sites, budget touched, caseload, jurisdictions, territory.
- Frequency and volume: per shift, per day, per term, per year.
- Labelled estimate: "roughly 25 to 30", "about a third of", "most".
- Before and after: what was true before the person acted and what was true afterwards, even if qualitative.
- Outside validation: awards, inspection results, ratings, being asked to train others, repeat clients, apprentices who passed.
- Consequence: what did not go wrong (safety record, audit, no rework, no complaints).
- Constraint: what made it hard (night shift, short staff, hazard, deadline, legacy system). Difficulty is evidence.
- Tier description under confidentiality: say the type of client or case and the shape of the result instead of names or figures.

Illustrations only (invented for teaching, not sourced; the person's real facts replace every detail):
- Nurse: "Charge nurse on a 24-bed cardiac step-down unit, 1:4 ratios on nights; precepted six new graduates, five stayed past their first year." Scope, ratio, outcome of a mentee group.
- Welder: "TIG and stick welding on pressure-vessel fabrication in confined spaces; work passed third-party inspection on the first attempt on the large majority of jobs; trained two apprentices to their certification test." Standard, constraint, inspection, apprentices.
- Teacher: "Taught four sections of Year 9 maths (about 110 pupils) including a streamed support group; replaced weekly worksheets with short low-stakes quizzes, and the support group's end-of-term results moved from below to level with the other sections." Class size, change, before and after.
- Lawyer: "Handled commercial lease disputes for mid-size retail tenants, matters from small claims to seven-figure exposure; two settled before trial after early mediation, which the client had not expected." Matter type and range, no client names.
- Hotel manager: "Ran front office and housekeeping for a 140-room city hotel at about 85% summer occupancy; rebuilt the shift handover so late-arrival complaints stopped appearing in the monthly review." Scope, volume, consequence.
- Software engineer: "Owned the billing service for a team of five, roughly 20,000 invoices a month; removed the manual month-end reconciliation by adding a nightly check, and incidents at close fell to none for two quarters." Scope, volume, before and after.
- Civil servant: "Drafted responses to ministerial correspondence for a housing policy unit, about 40 letters a month under a five-day deadline; introduced a shared tracker, and no reply went out late in the following year." Volume, constraint, consequence.

How to elicit: ask "what would have gone wrong if you had not been there", "how many, how often, how big", "who noticed and what did they say", "what was the hardest part". If every answer is "I do not know", write a scope-only line and say so; thin truthful text beats padded text.

## 15. Text that reads as generic or machine-written

No evidence shows that recruiters detect or penalise AI-written profile text, and LinkedIn documents no such penalty (nothing in LinkedIn's help or policies says otherwise). The practical cost is sounding like everyone else: ten profiles in a feed that read as one. The risk that does matter is untrue content, covered in the next section.

Tells:
- Interchangeable phrases: "proven track record of delivering impactful results", "passionate about driving innovation".
- Forced triplets and balanced pairs ("strategy, execution and impact"), stock verbs (leverage, spearhead, foster, drive).
- Uniform polished rhythm, every sentence the same length, no digression or rough edge.
- No proper nouns: no named tool, place, unit, client type or decision.
- About that does not match the timeline below it.
- Claims that nothing could falsify.

Fix: put in what only this person knows (a named system, a place, a decision, a mistake corrected, a number they can defend). Draft from their facts, in their own words, then cut: delete every sentence that would still be true of a colleague. If AI helped, reinsert the specifics after it has cut and clarified. Read the result aloud; if the person would not say it to a colleague over coffee, delete it. Match the person's real register and language variety; a profile in a market's own idiom beats a translated template.

## 16. The honesty ceiling

Emphasis is choosing which true things go first. Invention is adding things that are not true. The advisor never writes a fact the person did not supply: if a number, outcome, title or date is missing, ask or leave a visible placeholder, never fill it with a plausible guess. LinkedIn's rules require a profile to represent a real person and prohibit inflating Experience or Education [documented]; workplace verification is now required for recruiter job titles [documented, 2026].

- Titles: use the market equivalent of the title actually held, formal title alongside if different. Do not drop "assistant", "interim" or "contract", or turn a contractor role into employment without saying so.
- Dates: shorten or omit unflattering roles if the person prefers, but never falsify or stretch dates to hide a gap. Earliest start date drives years of experience in Recruiter [documented].
- Credentials: never list a licence, certificate or degree the person does not hold or that has lapsed; "in progress" must say so. In regulated professions it can also be unlawful.
- Numbers: only figures the person supplied; estimates labelled as estimates; anything they could not explain in an interview stays out.
- Credit: "led" versus "contributed to" matters. Sole credit for a team result is the commonest soft misrepresentation; use "with" or "on a team of".
- Photo: a good photo and light retouching are fine; one that no longer looks like the person, or an AI-generated face, is deception and is detected on the platform (secondary reports).
- Confidentiality: withholding figures under an NDA is honest; implying there was no constraint is not. Never publish patient, student or client details, including ones that could be reverse-identified.
- Open to Work: use it only if the person is looking. The Recruiters-only setting hides the signal from other members and tries to hide it from recruiters at the current employer, but LinkedIn says it cannot guarantee privacy [documented]; do not promise the person it is invisible to their employer. Contested: the public frame (some former recruiters call it a red flag, others report it helps, especially smaller employers); there is no solid data and profile quality matters more. Default: Recruiters only for people currently employed; public when visibility is the goal.
- AI assistance: using AI to tighten the person's own true content is fine; having AI generate roles, outcomes, skills or numbers is misrepresentation. Skills the person cannot demonstrate, and endorsements or recommendations bought or traded in bulk, stay out.
