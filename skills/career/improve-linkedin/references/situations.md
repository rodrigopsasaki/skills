# Situations: goal, stage, field and hard circumstances

Read this during "situate", before recommending anything, and whenever the person is not a mid-career office worker hunting a job. It tells you where standard advice breaks, how the reader and the failing gate shift by goal, stage and field, and when the honest answer is "LinkedIn is not where this decision gets made for you."
Facts as of November 2026; anything marked (check live) changes often. Untagged guidance is [practice].

## 1. Standard advice that does harm: checks to run first

Run these before any recommendation. Each is a place where the default playbook (complete everything, add keywords, quantify, post often, be open) injures a specific person. Ask the question in the left column; if the answer is yes, change the advice as shown.

1. **Is the person employed and hiding a search?**
   - Harm: the public Open to Work frame is visible to colleagues and the boss; a burst of posting, a full rewrite, a new photo or connection requests to recruiters all signal departure. The frame is shown to people at the current company, and LinkedIn says it cannot guarantee full privacy [documented].
   - Do instead: "Recruiters only" visibility, turn off "share profile updates" before editing (check live), edit in small steps, no frame. Never promise secrecy.
2. **Cleared, defence, intelligence or similar staff?**
   - Harm: maximising keywords, programmes, systems and clearance detail gives hostile services a targeting map. Foreign services are reported to use professional networks to approach cleared staff, and authorities have warned staff about advertising clearances (press and trade reporting, not a study). Whether to show a clearance level at all is [contested].
   - Do instead: describe the work at the level an unclassified brochure would; show clearance level at most, never programme, system or site; tell them the security officer (FSO or equivalent) and employer policy override any career advice, including yours. Accept lower search visibility.
3. **Regulated profession with protected titles or advertising rules (law above all)?**
   - Harm: "expert", "specialist", "best", practice-area lists, endorsements or recommendations can breach attorney advertising or title rules; some US bars treat profiles as advertising and require disclaimers; rules differ by state and country and the opinions are dated.
   - Do instead: ask which regulator applies and tell the person to read its current rules; describe the work factually, claim only licences and titles held in the relevant jurisdiction, avoid superlatives, watch endorsements others add.
4. **Does the work involve NDAs, patients, students, clients or sensitive customers?**
   - Harm: named clients and numbers breach contracts; patient or student detail breaches confidentiality even when anonymised if it can be reverse-identified.
   - Do instead: describe by industry, size and outcome ("a national retailer"), use ranges the person is allowed to share, and have them check the NDA or ask the employer. Not sharing a figure because of an NDA is honest; implying there was no constraint is not.
5. **Is the person older, or does one long chronology expose their age?**
   - Harm: listing every role and date invites age screening. Deleting all dates fixes that but can look evasive and breaks date-based sorting and sanity checks. See section 3 (late career); [contested], decide per audience.
   - Do instead: trim or collapse roles beyond roughly 15 years, keep dates on recent roles, keep education dates optional, show current tools. Never falsify or stretch dates.
6. **Are you about to press the person to disclose a gap, disability, health condition, transition or immigration status?**
   - Harm: the profile is public before any application, so disclosure is not "just a résumé line". Disclosure is a personal, per-employer decision and the evidence on outcomes is thin.
   - Do instead: offer the neutral options (one calm line, or nothing beyond what the profile must say), name the trade-off, and let them choose. Check photos, groups and volunteering entries for disclosure they did not intend.
7. **Is the headline a pivot with nothing behind it?**
   - Harm: claiming the target identity with no project, course, engagement or reference reads as hollow at the BELIEVED gate.
   - Do instead: put the target in the headline as direction ("moving from X to Y") or lead with the bridging skill, and ship evidence before escalating the claim.
8. **Does the person's real reader even use LinkedIn?**
   - Harm: "post consistently to build a brand" or "complete every section" burns effort for trades, teaching, frontline hospitality or retail, tenure-track academia, and anyone whose employer restricts public comms.
   - Do instead: do the minimum worth doing (section 6), point effort at the channel that carries the decision, and say so plainly.
9. **Is any statistic being used to justify the advice?**
   - Harm: many widely repeated numbers (social-selling score to win rate, share of buyers who check founders, share of recruiters on LinkedIn) trace to aggregator blogs. Do not use a number to persuade; use reasoning from how the reader behaves.

## 2. By goal

Block format: **Reader / Needs to see / Gate that usually fails / Do differently / Working looks like (and timing)**. No source found a measured timeline for any goal. Every timing below is [practice] or practitioner anecdote at best; say "no one has measured this" when asked.

### Active job search
- Reader: a recruiter running a search on LinkedIn, or a hiring manager checking a name after an application or referral.
- Needs to see: target title and level in the headline, matching keywords in headline, current title and Skills, recent outcomes.
- Failing gate: FOUND (wrong or missing keywords) then RECOGNISED (headline names the past, not the target).
- Differently: spend less on application volume and more on being findable and referable. LinkedIn reports applications per role roughly doubled since spring 2022 [vendor], which is why referral and recruiter paths matter more [inference]. The public frame is [contested]: LinkedIn-reported lift in recruiter contact [vendor]; a recruiter survey by a job-search vendor found few see it negatively [vendor]; some recruiters call it a weak-position signal [practice]. Common ground: the profile behind the frame matters more than the frame.
- Working: recruiter messages matching target title and level, profile views from target employers, interviews that cite the profile. Timing: weeks once keywords are right [practice]; no measured figure.

### Passive openness (employed, would listen)
- Reader: a recruiter or hiring manager sourcing people who are not applying.
- Needs to see: a current, accurate profile with recent outcomes and a clear niche; low-key reachability.
- Failing gate: ACTED ON (no signal that a message is welcome) and FOUND (stale profile).
- Differently: "Recruiters only" setting, which hides the signal from Recruiter users at your own company but is not guaranteed private [documented]. Quiet maintenance beats a rewrite. State preferences in the Open to Work details (titles, locations) instead of a public frame.
- Working: low volume, high relevance inbound. Timing: steady state over months [practice].

### Career change or pivot
- Reader: a recruiter or manager in the target field who has never seen this person; also the old-field network.
- Needs to see: target-role language, transferable outcomes, and some proof (project, course, engagement, volunteer work) in the target domain.
- Failing gate: FOUND (searches match past titles) and BELIEVED (claim without evidence). Over-claiming vs under-claiming the new identity is [contested].
- Differently: evidence usually has to precede the profile change. Reframe past work by outcomes the target reader values; add target-field skills only where demonstrable. LinkedIn's skills-based hiring framing is a direction of travel, not proof recruiter behaviour has changed [inference].
- Working: messages for the new role family appear and old-role messages fade. Timing: the slowest job goal; months, after evidence exists [practice].

### Return after a gap
- Reader: a recruiter or manager who sees a gap and decides whether it matters.
- Needs to see: a calm one-line explanation and proof of recent currency.
- Failing gate: BELIEVED (unexplained gap read as risk) or RECOGNISED (stale skills).
- Differently: LinkedIn offers a Career Break entry type with a set of reasons [documented]. Its own 2022 survey reports about half of hirers more likely to contact a candidate who explains a break and about one in five hiring managers rejecting candidates with breaks [vendor]. A separate vendor survey of US HR staff found most say gaps are expected, and about half say the reason matters [vendor]. Do not over-justify; add a course, volunteering or freelance work. Senior returners should look at returnship programmes.
- Working: interview invitations without the gap being the first question. Timing: weeks to months [practice].

### Promotion or internal visibility
- Reader: skip-level managers and cross-team colleagues, who mostly use internal channels.
- Needs to see: nothing on LinkedIn that they do not already see at work.
- Failing gate: the premise. LinkedIn is a weak lever here [practice, no study found].
- Differently: ask whether the goal is visibility to the employer or an exit option. If the former, advise internal channels and keep the profile accurate; recommendations from internal stakeholders can help; post only within comms policy. If the latter, treat as passive openness.
- Working: measured internally, not on LinkedIn. Timing: not applicable.

### Freelancing and consulting
- Reader: a buyer with a problem, often arriving through a referral or a post.
- Needs to see: the problem solved, niche, proof (case studies, client recommendations), the offer and a route to contact.
- Failing gate: RECOGNISED (résumé-style profile) and ACTED ON (no offer or contact route).
- Differently: write client-facing, not job-facing. Content and comments matter more than for job seekers because buyers judge thinking. The free Services page needs a described client problem and area served; richer features are paid (check live) [documented]. Marketplaces and referral networks may carry as much volume as LinkedIn for developers, designers and writers [practice].
- Working: inbound messages that describe a problem you solve, referrals that mention a post, views from buyer titles. Timing: practitioners claim first inquiries after a few months of steady posting and a reliable pipeline after half a year or more; all vendor or anecdote, no controlled data [practice].

### Founders
- Reader: one primary audience at a time: investor, candidate or customer.
- Needs to see: for investors, traction and domain credibility; for candidates, mission and accessibility; for buyers, problem focus.
- Failing gate: RECOGNISED (one profile trying to serve all three).
- Differently: choose the primary audience and write for it. Fundraising is mostly warm-intro mapping and diligence, not cold sourcing [practice]. Leave out circulating numbers on founder posting and lead lift; they have no traceable primary source.
- Working: investor, candidate or customer messages that cite the profile or posts. Timing: practitioner claims of several months; unmeasured [practice].

### Sales and business development
- Reader: a prospect or buyer who looks you up before a call.
- Needs to see: who you help, outcomes, customer proof, and credible expertise. In a LinkedIn and Ipsos buyer survey most buyers said seller expertise drives trust [vendor].
- Failing gate: BELIEVED (a buyer-irrelevant résumé) and ACTED ON.
- Differently: map warm introductions before outreach; LinkedIn claims warm intros yield more meetings [vendor]. Check employer social-media policy and brand rules first.
- Working: meetings booked from warm intros, buyers who arrive having read your profile. Timing: no figure.

### Recruiting and employer brand
- Reader: a passive candidate comparing you to other employers.
- Needs to see: roles, team, process, and a recruiter profile that reads as a person, not a template.
- Failing gate: ACTED ON (low reply rates) and RECOGNISED (generic outreach).
- Differently: the evidence base is mostly LinkedIn Talent Solutions and is self-interested; treat it as such. This area was not researched in depth; lean on the person's own response data.
- Working: candidate reply rate and quality of applicants. Timing: not researched.

### Thought leadership and speaking
- Reader: conference organisers, journalists, buyers.
- Needs to see: talks, publications, press, a speaker page; Featured section carries the proof.
- Failing gate: BELIEVED (opinions without evidence) and ACTED ON (no booking route).
- Differently: consistency beats frequency [practice]; exact schedules are unsupported. Distinct, evidence-backed views are said to differentiate against abundant generic AI-assisted content, but whether algorithm changes reward this is [contested]. Ask if the employer permits public commentary.
- Working: speaking invitations, press requests, follows from the target community. Timing: practitioners suggest 6 to 12 months; unmeasured [practice].

### Academics and researchers
- Reader: for academic careers, committees who use Google Scholar, ORCID and institutional pages. For leaving academia, industry recruiters.
- Needs to see: for exit, research translated into problems solved, methods and tools, with links to ORCID or Scholar.
- Failing gate: for academic goals, LinkedIn is not part of the decision (section 6). For exit, RECOGNISED.
- Differently: adoption studies find ResearchGate and Google Scholar lead, LinkedIn is secondary [measured] (small, dated, institution-specific). A study linking social-media presence to lower h-index is correlational only.
- Working: outside-academia contacts. Timing: not applicable for academic tracks.

### Minimal credible presence
- Reader: anyone who searches the person's name to verify them.
- Needs to see: a real photo, accurate current role, employer and education, spelled as on other records.
- Failing gate: BELIEVED only (a contradiction or absence that creates doubt; no measured penalty).
- Differently: set visibility deliberately and stop. A near-empty profile is acceptable where nobody looks.
- Working: a name search shows nothing surprising. Timing: immediate.

## 3. By stage

- **Student or new graduate.** Evidence is thin, so show coursework, projects, internships, volunteering and leadership. Network is the stated barrier: LinkedIn reports 44% of Gen Z cite lacking the right network [vendor], and that interns are more likely to hold full-time work within six months (correlational) [vendor]. Gate to fix: FOUND and RECOGNISED through school, program and target-role keywords. Add classmates, alumni and campus recruiters; ask for recommendations from supervisors. If the target field does not hire through LinkedIn (section 4), keep it minimal.
- **Early career.** Add first recommendations, move from duties to outcomes, tune search keywords. [practice]
- **Mid-career specialist.** Where most advice is written. Depth, niche keywords, evidence of impact. Standard playbook mostly applies.
- **Manager.** Show scope (team size, budget, outcomes) and people development; recommendations from reports and peers carry weight. [practice]
- **Executive and board.**
  - Retained search is network and client-assignment driven, not keyword driven; at least one major firm says its consultants contact you only if a client assignment matches (per the firm's own site).
  - The public profile is a diligence artefact: consistent narrative, board and governance experience, press and speaking, restrained tone. Frequent posting is optional and can hurt if off-brand. [practice; weakly sourced]
  - Check for contradictions between profile, company bio and press, since reference checks start there.
- **Late career.**
  - Evidence of age bias: a 2025 academic study (Schellaert, Oostrom, Derous) is reported to show bias against older applicants in LinkedIn-style screening, with some positive competence stereotypes; it is known here only second-hand, so treat it as [contested] until read at source. A hiring-manager survey reports 42% consider age [vendor]. A blog-reported claim that older users get fewer offers despite stronger profiles is unverified.
  - The dates question: advice to drop graduation dates and trim roles past about 15 years is common [practice]. Counter-risk: removing dates can look evasive and disables chronological checks [contested]. Compromise: keep dates on recent roles, trim old roles to one line, omit education dates, never alter dates.
  - Photo: sources conflict between "a younger-looking photo helps" and "no photo looks out of touch" [contested]. Advise a current, professional photo.
  - Show currency: recent tools, certifications, a recent learning signal.
- **Encore.** Goal shifts to advisory, board, mentoring, interim or fractional work. State the new offer, not the old chronology. [practice; light sourcing]

## 4. By field

Field-level usage data is largely absent. Everything here is [practice] or [inference] unless tagged. "LinkedIn starts to matter" means the move into leadership, corporate, or out of the field.

- **Healthcare and nursing.**
  - Who looks: hospital and agency recruiters, travel-nursing agencies, health-system talent teams.
  - What counts: licences, board certifications, state, specialty. LinkedIn has no dedicated field for some of these, so use the headline and Licenses and Certifications.
  - Real channel: employer career sites, job boards, agency portals [practice, not verified].
  - Cautions: no patient information; protected titles (nurse, physician, psychologist) must match the licence.
  - Matters more for: leadership, informatics, pharma, health-tech.
- **Skilled trades.**
  - Who looks: commercial clients, general contractors, and recruiters for estimating, project management or safety roles.
  - What counts: licences, certifications, apprenticeship, safety record, project photos.
  - Real channel: referrals, union halls, trade and general job boards. Sources disagree on LinkedIn's value for tradespeople [contested]; LinkedIn says a large share of its users are first-line workers [vendor] but tradespeople report little benefit.
  - Matters more for: owner-operators courting commercial clients, and moves into estimating, PM, safety or contracting.
- **Teaching.**
  - Who looks: little evidence of use by districts. [inference]
  - Real channel: district application systems and state certification databases [practice, not verified].
  - What counts: certification, subjects, grades taught.
  - Matters more for: administrators and exits into ed-tech, instructional design, learning and development.
- **Law.**
  - Who looks: clients, referral lawyers, recruiters.
  - What counts: bar admissions, practice areas stated factually, representative matters at a level confidentiality allows.
  - Cautions: section 1 item 3. Bar opinions (for example New York City 2015-7, Michigan, Rhode Island) differ and are dated; check current rules.
- **Public sector and military transition.**
  - Who looks: contractor and private-sector recruiters, SkillBridge hosts; federal hiring itself runs through structured government systems.
  - What counts: civilian-equivalent titles, quantified outcomes, translated acronyms.
  - Cautions: clearance (section 1 item 2). Veteran programmes such as free Premium offers change; (check live).
  - Matters more for: the transition itself, where LinkedIn is the networking and translation layer.
- **Creative and portfolio work.**
  - Who looks: recruiters and art directors who start on LinkedIn then go to the portfolio.
  - What counts: case studies showing process, linked from Featured. The claim that most recruiters spend minutes on a portfolio is a blog statistic with no audit and should not be repeated.
  - Real channel: the portfolio, Behance, Dribbble, referrals; whether those suffice for senior product or UX roles is [contested].
- **Hospitality and retail.**
  - Who looks: for frontline work, little evidence.
  - Real channel: employer career sites, job boards, local walk-ins [practice, not verified].
  - Matters more for: management, hotel-group corporate, moves into corporate roles.
- **Nonprofits.**
  - Who looks: funders, board members, peers; sector job boards also matter [inference].
  - What counts: mission and outcome evidence in plain language, not corporate vocabulary.
- **Science and academia.**
  - See "Academics and researchers" in section 2. In industry R&D, publications, patents and conference output count; recruiters read LinkedIn.
- **Regulated professions generally.**
  - Principle: do not claim licences, titles or designations not held in the relevant jurisdiction, and do not use "specialist" or "expert" where certification schemes govern them. Financial advisers, health professions, accountants and engineers have title or communications rules that vary by place [practice]. Ask which regulator applies and have the person check it. Fabricated credentials risk dismissal and legal action.
- **Software engineering.**
  - Who looks: recruiters source heavily on LinkedIn, and also check GitHub, open source, writing and referrals.
  - What counts: collaboration quality and substance of work over repository counts [practice].
  - Differently: senior engineers often have thin profiles, which hurts search visibility. They also receive heavy low-quality recruiter volume, so the aim is selectivity (stack, location, compensation floor) not more inbound. "Share of recruiters on LinkedIn" statistics are unaudited.

## 5. Hard situations

| Employment gap | Use a Career Break entry or one calm line; do not blur years to hide it. Add recent currency. | [documented] feature, [vendor] on effect |
| Short tenures | Group contract or startup stints under one entry; state the reason briefly (contract end, closure, layoff). Little LinkedIn-specific data. | [practice] |

| Many short contracts | One umbrella entry ("Independent consultant") with a line per engagement; recent detail only. | [practice] |

| Job hunting while employed | "Recruiters only", no frame, no update broadcasts. Not guaranteed private. | [documented] |

## 6. LinkedIn is not the channel: the respectable outcome

Sometimes the right advice is: "LinkedIn is not where this decision gets made for you. Here is the minimum worth doing, and here is where your effort is better spent." Say it without apology when you find these.
- **Where it applies** [practice, with thin data]: trades hiring by referral and local boards; teachers hired through district portals; most frontline hospitality, retail and clinical roles; tenure, grant and academic hiring (committees use Scholar, ORCID, institutional pages); federal applications; people whose employer bars public comms; people hiding a search.
- **The minimum worth doing:** the minimal credible presence in section 2: real photo, accurate role, employer and education, consistent with other records, visibility set on purpose. Thirty minutes; then stop.
- **Redirect effort:** name the actual channel (referrals, union hall, district portal, publication record, portfolio, a regulator-compliant practice page) and one next action there.
- **Review triggers:** tell them LinkedIn becomes worth revisiting when they move toward leadership, corporate roles, consulting for businesses, or out of the field.
- Do not oversell absence of evidence: "no study shows LinkedIn helps teachers" is not "LinkedIn hurts teachers." Say you are reasoning from where the hiring decision visibly happens [inference].

## 7. Evidence quality

Almost every number in this area comes from LinkedIn about its own users or from vendor surveys, and LinkedIn has an interest in activity. Independent measurement is close to absent: the main exceptions are small adoption studies of academics and one 2025 ageism study known here only second-hand. Nothing found measures time-to-result for any goal, or LinkedIn outcomes for teaching, trades, hospitality, retail, nonprofits, visa situations, gender transition, overqualification or executive search. Where the research said "from background knowledge, not verified", this file carries it as [practice] or [inference] and never as fact. When asked, say "practitioners generally say X; no one has measured it." Primary documents worth fetching if a decision depends on them: LinkedIn's 2022 career-break survey, the 2025 ageism paper, LinkedIn Economic Graph disability reports, and current bar and regulator rules.
